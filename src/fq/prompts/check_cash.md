4. **Net cash.** This company holds net cash (cash and short-term investments less all debt, on the shareholders' share) of at least {{CASH_MIN}}% of its market value: the one figure over a price you are given. Read it as a long-term minority shareholder would: net cash is the owners' asset and a margin of safety, not a fault in itself. Two questions decide it. **Is it really the owners'?** Look for the catch: cash trapped abroad or blocked by capital controls; regulatory, customer or deposit money; a controlling shareholder or parent that keeps it or takes it out on its own terms; pending liabilities, litigation, guarantees or a large committed outlay. **Does it reach them?** Use "What the owners were paid" (dividends plus buybacks against free cash flow, and the share count) and the long dividend record. Mark the topic by these rules:
- **ok**: the cash is clean, and it is shared or needed: dividends plus buybacks took about a third or more of free cash flow over the years shown, or buybacks cut the share count, or the cash has a named need (a capital cycle, a funded expansion, working capital that swings). A large pile that is being returned is ok, however large.
- **caution**: the cash is clean but piling up: it grows faster than the business while dividends plus buybacks stay under about a third of free cash flow and the share count does not fall, with no stated use.
- **fail** (a real catch): the cash is not really the minority shareholders' (client, regulatory, deposit or insurance money; trapped by capital controls or abroad; offset by pending liabilities, litigation or a committed outlay), or a controlling family or parent has a record of related-party leakage or value-destroying acquisitions, or the cash has been hoarded for years while dividends and buybacks stay negligible and the cash earns little.

The line follows the mark: an ok line says why the cash is fine for the owners; a caution line starts with the concern. Many conservative balance sheets, common in Japan, Korea and family companies, are ok when they pay out and a caution when they do not: fail only on a real catch.
