4. **Net cash.** This company holds net cash (cash and short-term investments less all debt, on the shareholders' share) of at least {{CASH_MIN}}% of its market value: the one figure over a price you are given, because so much idle cash is itself a question. Why is it not used? A company that can reinvest at a high return on capital should; one that cannot should pay it out; one whose shares trade below what they are worth should buy them back. Look for the catch: cash trapped abroad or blocked by capital controls; regulatory, customer or deposit money that is not really the owners'; a controlling shareholder or parent that keeps it or takes it out on its own terms; pending liabilities, litigation, guarantees or a large planned outlay; a record of spending cash badly. Say whether the cash is really available to minority shareholders and whether it is likely to be used well, and mark the topic by these rules:
- **fail** (a real catch): the cash is not really the minority shareholders' (client, regulatory, deposit or insurance money; trapped by capital controls or abroad; offset by pending liabilities, litigation or a committed outlay), or a controlling family or parent has a record of related-party leakage or value-destroying acquisitions, or the cash has been hoarded for years while dividends and buybacks stay negligible and the cash earns little.
- **caution**: the cash is clean and unencumbered, but the balance sheet is conservatively over-capitalised and the payout only modest, with no stated use.
- **ok**: the cash is clean and is being returned through a reasonable dividend or buybacks, or it has a plausible need (a capital cycle, a funded expansion, working capital that swings).

Many conservative balance sheets, common in Japan, Korea and family companies, are a caution, not a fail: fail only on a real catch.
